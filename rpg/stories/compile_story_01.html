PART ONE

The Woman Who Asked

Every Signal Runner knows the name. Most know the story. Almost none know the woman.

Compile was already blind when the first Signal Runner decks began appearing in the sprawl. No useful retinal implant. No visual overlay. No neural rendering system worth trusting. The clinics kept telling her that better hardware was coming — a next-generation cortical patch, a finer-grained optic bridge, always another year out.

She stopped waiting.

Her equipment, by any standard the sprawl respected, was unimpressive: a terminal, an old braille display, a keyboard, a handful of ordinary command-line tools. Nothing rare. Nothing stolen from a corp lab. What she had that nobody else quite did was an increasingly refined habit of asking questions — one at a time, in order, never skipping ahead on a hunch.

That habit became a methodology. The methodology became a discipline. The discipline, passed hand to hand over the years that followed, became the foundation the Dead Reckoning Line still runs on.

PART ONE, CONTINUED

The First Principle

Compile rejected the basic assumption every visual netrunner in the city built their whole practice on:

If you can see the system, you understand the system.

She considered this not just wrong but actively dangerous. A rendered network could show topology while hiding ownership. An illuminated process tree could show activity while hiding causality. A beautiful visualization — and the corps built genuinely beautiful ones — could make an unfamiliar system feel familiar long before it actually was. That feeling, she argued, killed more runners than any ICE ever had.

She wanted something smaller instead. Something testable. She would begin with exactly one question, then build the smallest query capable of answering it — nothing more. The answer became the boundary for the next question. Nothing was assumed because it looked plausible. Nothing was accepted just because a tool reported it. The system was interrogated one fact at a time, and her notebooks from those years fill page after page with sequences like this one:

What am I looking at?

What owns it?

Who uses it?

Where is it defined?

What reaches it?

What does it reach?

What changes it?

What happens if it fails?

What remains unaccounted for?

To other runners watching her work, this looked painfully slow. Compile called it something else: reducing the search space. She wasn't slower because she was cautious. She was slower because she refused to spend a single query on a question she hadn't actually finished asking.

THE JOB THAT MADE THE NAME

The RUSTCORE Vault

The job that made her famous started as nothing special — one contract among dozens moving through the Line that season. A Megacorp had built a restricted RUSTCORE vault and was advertising it, loudly, as effectively unreachable.

Its security architecture leaned almost entirely on visual disruption. The design assumption underneath it was simple, and by every metric the corp had tested, reliable:

A confused operator makes mistakes.

The vault generated visual noise on demand — shifting representations, false pathways, recursive overlays, threat indicators that changed faster than a human eye could track. It wasn't subtle. It didn't need to be. It was built around the one sensory channel every previous intrusion attempt had relied on.

Compile simply removed that channel from the equation.

She went in through a terminal. No rendered cyberspace. No artificial skyline. No glowing ICE waiting to overwhelm eyes she didn't use to navigate in the first place. Just text, and the same disciplined sequence she'd been running for years: one query, one answer, one narrowed field, repeat.

The first query established the entry point. The second established ownership. The third traced a dependency nobody on the vault's design team had documented — and by the time its security system finally registered an anomaly worth flagging, Compile was already somewhere else. Not geographically. Conceptually. She had stopped treating the vault as a maze the moment she walked in, and started treating it as what it actually was: a program. A program has states, inputs, dependencies, boundaries — and if you can pin those down precisely enough, the apparent complexity around them simply collapses.

WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED

The Three-Question Incident

The legend says she breached the vault in three questions. That's probably not literally true — but the surviving logs suggest something more interesting than the legend anyway.

She asked three major questions. Each one contained a chain of smaller investigations underneath it, the same reducing-the-search-space discipline from her notebooks, just scaled up to the size of a hostile mainframe instead of a single file. What the three major questions actually established was this:

  • The first established where she actually was.
  • The second established what controlled what.
  • The third established which assumption in the security model was false.

The vault didn't fail because Compile found some magic backdoor nobody else had noticed. It failed because its designers had built their entire security posture around a model of the operator — and that model simply didn't describe the person now inside it. Compile found the model. Then she stepped outside it, and there was nothing left in the vault's design that accounted for what came next.
