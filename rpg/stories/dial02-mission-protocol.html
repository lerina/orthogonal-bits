The File That Wasn't About Code

The sponsor's message came through two days after Dial closed out the training repo clean. No congratulations — the Line didn't really do those. Just a second file, smaller than the first, and a single line above the login: the terminal manual teaches you to find things. This one teaches you to finish them.

Dial opened it expecting more commands. There weren't any. Just a shape — eight letters, spelling the one word Dial had been hearing since the vouching went through, that they'd never once thought to read as instructions.

THE FILE, IN FULL

CAPSLOCK Quick Rules: The Mission Protocol

An upgrade to Quest Deconstruction (core rulebook, Section 12). Same six-step spine, plus a setup line and a verification step the original was missing — and the whole thing now spells the game's own name.

Before You Start — The Brief

Three lines, optional but recommended for anything non-trivial:

  • Mission: What am I actually doing?
  • Win condition: What does "complete" look like?
  • Status: Where am I starting from right now?

This is the part that makes the Protocol work equally well on a boss fight, a broken Rust test, and a sink full of dishes — it forces the vague feeling of "I should do something" into a sentence you can check against later.

DIAL'S LOG — NIGHT FIVE

Ran the Brief on the first thing in front of me before I even understood the rest of the file: the pile of laundry I'd been stepping over for four days. Mission — get it done. Win condition — basket empty, clothes put away. Status — currently the reason I can't find my other boot. Took eleven seconds to write. Felt stupid writing it. Did the laundry anyway, first time in a week without arguing with myself about it first.

THE PROTOCOL

C-A-P-S-L-O-C-K

C

Checkable Target

State the concrete win condition in one sentence. Not "clean the kitchen," but "counter's clear and the sink is empty." If you can't check it, it isn't a target yet.

A

Acknowledge the Obstacle

Name exactly what's standing between you and the target. No obstacle named means it isn't a mission — it's just a fact sitting there.

P

Penalties (the Stakes)

What actually gets worse if this is skipped? A real cost, not a vague one. No real cost means the target's not worth a mission — shrink it or drop it.

S

Select the Adversary

Cast the obstacle by how it's behaving right now, not by what kind of task it technically is.

Watcher — avoidance, hesitation, awkwardness. Tactic: circumvent it. Slip past quietly, start before it notices.
Enforcer — resistance that just takes sustained effort. Tactic: focus fire. Push straight through with the smallest real push.
Hunter — too big or too dangerous to face head-on. Tactic: kite and chip. Don't confront it directly — wear it down, or route around it entirely.

The same task can be a different adversary on a different day. Washing dishes is a Watcher when you're just avoiding starting, and an Enforcer once your arms are actually tired. Cast the obstacle as experienced, not as labeled.

L

Launch the First Move

One physical, observable action. Small enough to start inside two minutes. This is the only step you're required to know before you begin.

O

Observe & Verify

Produce actual evidence the target's been hit — a fact, not a feeling. cargo test passes. The counter's clear and the sink's empty. The email shows as sent. If you can't point to the evidence, you're not done, you just stopped.

C

Claim the Reward

Immediate, not big — delivered the instant O confirms. A scene resolved, a Glitch cleared, a coffee, a mental checkmark. The point is speed, so it registers as done instead of still open.

K

Kill the Mission

Once verified, stop. Don't keep polishing a mission that's already complete — that's not diligence, it's a new mission wearing the old one's name. If you want to keep going, that's Checkable Target, step one, again, honestly.

Activity → Target → Obstacle → Stakes → Adversary → Tactic → First Move → Verification → Reward → Kill
DIAL'S LOG

K was the one that got me. I don't stop. Sponsor already knew that about me before I did, I think — why else would they have made it the last letter of the recruiter's own name. Read it four times before I let myself believe it was actually a rule and not just a suggestion I was allowed to argue with.

A WEEK LATER

The First One That Wasn't Practice

The job itself was small — small enough that the sponsor almost didn't bother routing it through the Line at all. A contact needed a name confirmed inside a firm's low-security personnel directory, nothing RUSTCORE would ever notice, nothing worth a real Rig. But it was real. Someone was actually going to use what Dial found.

DIAL'S LOG — FIRST REAL RUN

Brief. Mission: confirm the name. Win condition: name and role verified against two independent fields. Status: I have a company name and nothing else.

C. Target: the employee's current role, confirmed. A. Obstacle: directory's not public, needs a foothold. P. Stakes: contact's next move depends on this being right, not fast. S. Cast it Watcher — nothing here is going to fight back, I just have to not be noticed finding it. L. First move: open the terminal, rg the leaked directory dump for the surname.

Found it in four minutes using exactly what the first file taught me. Text search, then context, then done — never even needed ast-grep. O. Two independent fields matched. C. Reward: told the sponsor it was done before I let myself second-guess it. K. Closed the terminal. Didn't reopen it to check a third time. That part was the actual hard part.

D I A L   ·   F I R S T   M I S S I O N

Two files, five nights, one small job that mattered anyway. Dial still has both documents open in separate panes most nights — one for finding things, one for finishing them. Nobody on the Line has told them yet that most runners never actually stop needing both.
