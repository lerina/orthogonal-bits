CAPSLOCK

A No‑Dice, No‑Sheet Cyberpunk RPG

A parlor RPG built for walks, drives, and idle moments — no dice, no paper, no rulebook. Just conversation, memory, and a handful of mental rules.

1. Core Philosophy

CAPSLOCK runs on three principles:

  • Everything fits in your head. No stat blocks, no tracking sheets. If it can't be remembered in a sentence, it's too complicated.
  • Consequences over numbers. Nothing has hit points. Things are either fine, Glitched, or gone — and failure always pushes the story forward instead of stopping it.
  • The world only exists where you're looking. Nothing is planned in advance. You invent the next room the moment you turn the corner.

2. Core Resolution System

CAPSLOCK borrows its randomizer from classic diceless play (à la Sherpa) and its stakes-betting spirit from Baron Munchausen, filtered through a simple "Yes, But / No, And" engine.

The Watch Method (your random number generator)

  1. The player states their action. I try to hack the terminal before the guard rounds the corner.
  2. The GM silently picks a Difficulty from 1–3 (Easy / Medium / Hard) and thinks of a secret number in that range.
  3. The player calls a number in the same range.
  4. Resolve:
    • Match → Clean success.
    • Miss, low stakes → Yes, but… — success with a complication.
    • Miss, high stakes → No, and… — failure that escalates the scene.

No sheet, no dice — just a number held in your head for three seconds.

Optional variant: use the second hand of a digital watch or phone clock instead of a "secret number" — call whether it'll land in the first, second, or third 20‑second band of the minute.

3. Character Creation

Characters are built from three portable pieces: Archetype, Gear, and Cyberware — plus a new fourth piece, Style, which gives every character a distinct voice without adding anything to track.

The Rule of Three

Every character carries:

  • 3 Gear Slots
  • 2 Cyberware Tags
  • 1 Style

That's it. If you can't remember your whole character in one breath, trim it down.

Archetypes

Style — Give Your Character a Voice

Style is one phrase describing how your character does things, not what they do. It costs nothing to track and colors every roll.

Pick one, or invent your own:

  • Reckless — you narrate your successes bigger and louder than they need to be.
  • Cold & Calculated — you narrate failures as "part of the plan."
  • Charismatic Wreck — every social success comes with a self‑inflicted complication.
  • Old‑School — you distrust chrome; you solve problems with skill before cyberware.
  • Corpo Defector — you always know the correct jargon, badge, or protocol to fake your way past something.

Mechanically, Style earns you exactly one thing: once per session, if your narration truly embodies your Style, the GM upgrades a "Yes, but" into a clean "Yes."

The "Always On" Rule

Cyberware never burns resources. It's a permanent narrative advantage — always active, no charge to track.

Burn‑Out Slots

Gear and cyberware have one state each: Functional or Glitched. A bad hit or a pushed‑too‑hard use flips it to Glitched. It stays useless until a narrative fix — a back‑alley ripperdoc, a scavenged part, a quiet hour of repair.

4. NEW: The Netrunning Rig

Hacking gets its own lightweight mini‑system so digital infiltration feels distinct from physical action, without adding tracking.

Your Rig is one sentence, chosen at character creation (Infiltrators start with one free; anyone can jack a rig from a corpse or black market for a Gear slot):

  • A scavenged military cyberdeck — brutal and fast, but loud.
  • A boutique stealth deck — quiet, but underpowered against military ICE.
  • A jury‑rigged street deck — cheap, glitchy, and impossible to trace.

Your Rig's flavor determines what a "Yes, but" costs you when netrunning:

  • Loud rigs → complications draw attention (a nearby Watcher notices).
  • Underpowered rigs → complications take time (the guard patrol gets closer).
  • Untraceable rigs → complications hit you physically (feedback burns your neural port).

Diving In: Entering a system is a single roll against the Watch Method. Success gets you in. A "No, and" means the ICE spots you immediately — treat it as waking a Hunter.

Inside the Net: Don't map the system. Describe only the one node you're touching right now (a door, a camera feed, a data cache) — Fog of War applies to cyberspace exactly like the street.

Burning Out: Your Rig can Glitch like any other gear. A Glitched Rig doesn't just stop working — it locks you out of your own head for a beat: describe a jarring sensory blackout before you can act again.

5. World & Lore Rules

You don't need a setting bible. Build the world exactly as far as your character can currently see.

Rule 1 — One Corp, One Gang, One Bar

Your entire setting only needs three standing entities:

  • The Megacorp — the faceless, city‑spanning enemy (Arasaka, Weyland, Tyrell — pick a vibe).
  • The Street Gang — the immediate local threat (The Neon Skulls, The Chromers).
  • The Safe Zone — the one place your character can breathe (The Noodle Bar, The Safehouse).

Rule 2 — The Rule of Threes (Sensory Anchors)

Any new location gets exactly three sensory details: one light, one sound, one smell. A neon pink sign flickering, the hum of a server rack, the smell of cheap synthetic noodles. Your brain fills in the rest automatically.

Rule 3 — Progressive Generation (Fog of War)

Nothing exists until it's looked at. Don't pre‑plan a building's layout. When your character turns a corner, decide then what's there — camera, guard, or empty hallway.

Rule 4 — "Who Profits?" (Instant Lore)

Any lore question — how the internet works, how policing works, why the sky is smog‑orange — gets answered by asking which Megacorp profits from the current state of things. Why is the air toxic? Because BioGen sells the masks.

6. Gear — Expanded Ideas

Gear should be evocative, not itemized. A few extra options beyond the archetype defaults, sortable by Slot:

  • Weapon Slot: railgun pistol · monofilament whip · EMP grenade (shuts down one piece of tech, no roll needed) · improvised molotov
  • Utility Slot: grapple‑hook launcher · portable signal jammer · black‑market med‑stim (clears one Glitch instantly, once) · lockpick multitool
  • Disguise/Social Slot: stolen corp uniform · forged biometric patch · a favor owed by a fixer · a convincing sob story

Keep the total at three. Swapping gear mid‑session means narrating how you lost or traded the old piece.

9. Story Hooks

Pick one, or draw from all three across a session:

1. The Broken Uplink — Trapped in a Megacorp server room's maintenance shaft with a data drive overheating your neural port on a mental timer, you must bluff past a patrol drone with a stolen uniform and a glitching voice modulator.

2. The Black Market Drop — Mid‑deal for a prototype cyber‑eye in a crowded noodle bar, a warning flashes across your vision: armed corporate agents just walked in the front door. Escape without drawing attention.

3. The Neon Chase — You've stolen a luxury flying vehicle, but its autopilot is fighting your controls while interceptor drones close in through smog‑choked skyscraper gaps.

10. Quick Session Setup

Fill in these four blanks before you start walking/driving, and the whole game is loaded in your head:

  • Archetype: Infiltrator / Street Samurai / Face / custom
  • Style: one phrase
  • Rig flavor: loud / underpowered / untraceable
  • Story Hook: The Broken Uplink / The Black Market Drop / The Neon Chase / custom

11. Running the Scene — The Storytelling Backbone

This is the mental shape that turns a Story Hook into an actual scene, without any prep. Use it as the GM's internal skeleton while you narrate.

The idea: The Hook gives you a beginning and an ending. Everything that makes it a story — why the player cares, the twist, the moment the plan breaks — lives in the middle. Don't think of the middle as something to "fill." Think of it as pressure you build through, one decision at a time.

The shape (used at two zoom levels):

  • Whole session: Premise → Intrigue → Conflict → Outcome
  • Any single beat, zoomed in: Attention → Tension → Transformation → Outcome

Same four steps, twice. If one moment in a scene needs more weight, zoom into it and run the four steps again inside it — that's the only "trick" to the whole system.

Five questions for when a scene stalls

  1. What do they stand to lose that isn't their life? — dignity, a promise, a contact, not just survival.
  2. What's their best flaw and their worst virtue? — force a choice between two good things; whatever they don't pick comes back later in the run.
  3. What can't be solved by the skill they're proudest of? — let the plan work exactly as intended, then let the side effect of that success be the real problem.
  4. What truth would end things if said out loud? — trap two characters in a small space right after it almost got said (an elevator, a getaway car).
  5. What trope are you already running? (Heist gone wrong, chosen one, found family…) — break one rule of it on purpose.

How to connect scenes instead of "and then"

  • Cause: Which meant that… / What nobody saw coming was…
  • Feeling: Because she couldn't bear to… / The thing he'd never told anyone was…
  • Echo: Which was exactly like the time… — bring a small detail back later, changed.
  • Irony: The very thing that saved her then would destroy her now.
  • Pace: One heartbeat. Two. Then— or Just when she thought it was over, it began again.
  • The one that does everything at once: Which is why, later, when…

One rule for sensory detail: this folds directly into Rule 2 (Sensory Anchors) from Section 5 — pick exactly one dominant sense per scene (sound, body/tension, temperature, internal/heartbeat, overload‑noise, or touch), plus at most one small secondary touch. Never stack more than that.

That's the whole narration layer: a repeating four‑step shape, five questions for when you're stuck, six ways to link scenes, and one sense per scene.

12. Quest Deconstruction — Turning Any Target into a Quest

This is the utility method underneath every Story Hook: a six‑step way to take any activity or target — an NPC's goal, a session objective, or something in real life you're gamifying on the walk itself — and shape it into something with actual pull. It reuses the Adversary vocabulary from Section 7, so a fictional obstacle and a real one get sorted the same way.

  1. Name the Target. State it as one concrete sentence with a checkable end state. Not clean the apartment, but the kitchen counter is clear and the sink is empty. Not steal the data, but the drive is in your pocket and the terminal shows no alert.
  2. Find the Obstacle. Ask why this hasn't already happened. There's always something in the way — low energy, a missing tool, a locked door, a guard rotation. Name it explicitly. No obstacle means it isn't a quest, it's just a fact.
  3. Set the Stakes. What actually gets worse if this is skipped? Not doom — a real cost (the dishes pile up, the patrol tightens, the trust erodes). If you can't name a cost, the target isn't worth questing over — shrink it or drop it.
  4. Pick One Adversary Type. Cast the obstacle as a Watcher (something to avoid or slip past quietly), an Enforcer (something to push through with effort or Gear), or a Hunter (something too big to face head‑on — needs a workaround). This single choice tells you what kind of approach the quest calls for.
  5. Define the First Move. Not the whole plan — just the next physical action, small enough to start in under two minutes. Open the terminal. Put on the stealth suit. Pick up the first dish. It's the only step you're required to know before you begin.
  6. Attach a Reward. Something delivered the instant the target is hit — a scene resolved, a Glitch cleared, a coffee, a mental checkmark. It doesn't need to be big, just immediate, so it registers as done rather than still open.

Quick example — real life: Reply to that overdue email. Target: reply sent, thread closed. Obstacle: it's awkward, so you keep avoiding it. Stakes: the longer it sits, the more awkward the reply gets. Adversary type: Watcher — nothing to fight, just something to face and get past quietly. First move: open the draft. Reward: archive the thread the second it sends.

Quick example — in‑fiction: Get past the rooftop patrol drone. Target: you're on the far ledge, undetected. Obstacle: the drone's scan sweep every 20 seconds. Stakes: getting spotted wakes the building's Hunter. Adversary type: Watcher. First move: time the sweep. Reward: the scene cuts straight to the next room, no roll needed for the crossing itself.
