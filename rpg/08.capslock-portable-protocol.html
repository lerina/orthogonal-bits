The Document That Doesn't Need the Line

Every other file that circulates on the Dead Reckoning Line assumes something — a deck, a terminal, a Rig, a body that can at least reach a keyboard. This one doesn't. Four things carried, all of them already in your head before you ever heard of RUSTCORE: a name, a want, a city, a voice. No hardware to source, no software to configure, nothing a sponsor has to vouch you into.

That's exactly why it didn't stay contained to Signal Runners for long. Copies turn up in places nothing else from the Line ever reaches — taped inside lockers, read aloud on long commutes, passed between people who've never touched a cyberdeck and never intend to. It isn't doctrine anymore, not really. It's closer to something the whole city quietly agreed to share.

THE DOCUMENT, IN FULL

CAPSLOCK ⠠⠏ — The Portable Voice-First Mission Protocol

CAPSLOCK is a solo narrative game played through voice, movement, sensory awareness, and imagination. It is not a game you sit down to prepare. It is a protocol you carry into ordinary life. A commute, a chore, a walk, a programming task, or a moment before sleep can become a mission.

You carry only four things:

  • A character name.
  • A character's want.
  • A city.
  • A voice.

Everything else emerges during play. The real environment provides sensory anchors. The voice creates conflict. Your actions advance the story. The goal is not to invent a complicated game world. The goal is to make the next moment playable.

SECTION 1

The Mental Save-File

At any moment, you should be able to express the current state in one sentence:

(Character) wants (checkable end state) in (city), while (voice) says (doubt).

Example:

Mara wants to reach the river before midnight in the flooded city, while the voice says she is already too late.

This is your portable save-file. You do not need inventory, statistics, character sheets, or written notes. If a detail matters, attach it to a sensory anchor, a physical landmark, or a recurring phrase.

Examples:

  • Tense shoulders represent danger.
  • A red door represents a safehouse.
  • A familiar sound represents an approaching threat.
  • A recurring phrase represents the voice's influence.

These associations are optional. Use only what you can comfortably remember. Forgetting is allowed. You can resume from the last remembered want, landmark, or unresolved question.

SECTION 2

The CAPSLOCK ⠠⠏ Protocol

CAPSLOCK is an eight-part internal monologue. You do not need to answer every part during every scene. Use the parts that help you move.

C

Check the End State

What is the character trying to accomplish right now? State the goal as a concrete, checkable outcome.

  • Reach the next intersection.
  • Find the hidden entrance.
  • Clear the kitchen counter.
  • Understand why a Rust function is called.
  • Complete the next paragraph.

A mission needs a recognizable outcome, even if it is small.

A

Acknowledge the Obstacle

What stands in the way? Name the obstacle without requiring an immediate solution.

  • The character is hesitating.
  • The path is blocked.
  • The code's behavior is unclear.
  • The task feels too large.
  • The voice is creating doubt.

Acknowledgment makes the obstacle available for action.

P

Pick an Adversary

Choose one adversary type that matches the obstacle's current behavior. The three adversaries are Watcher, Enforcer, and Hunter.

  • Watcher — a passive barrier, avoidance pattern, awkward situation, or mental block. Creates hesitation rather than direct resistance. Tactic: Circumvent. Take a small direct action instead of over-preparing.
  • Enforcer — requires sustained physical or mental effort, resisting until you apply enough of it. Tactic: Focus fire. Work a limited portion for a defined period; manageable effort, not maximum exertion.
  • Hunter — too large, complex, or overwhelming to confront directly. Punishes an all-at-once approach. Tactic: Kite and chip. Reduce the problem to a manageable piece, secure progress, reassess.

The adversary describes the obstacle's behavior in the current moment. The same task can become a different adversary later.

S

Set the Stakes

What worsens if the character fails or delays? Choose a real or fictional consequence.

  • The deadline approaches.
  • The character loses time.
  • A clue becomes harder to recover.
  • The dishes continue to accumulate.
  • The character must find another route.

Keep the stakes proportional to the activity. A small mission can have small stakes. A scene can also have no immediate penalty if exploration is the point. The stakes create meaning, not pressure for its own sake.

L

Launch the First Move

What is the smallest useful action the character can take now? Choose a concrete action that can begin immediately.

  • Open the file.
  • Read the next function.
  • Pick up one dish.
  • Take the next safe step.
  • Ask the voice one question.
  • Search for one clue.

You only need to know the first move. The rest of the mission can emerge through play.

O

Observe the Result

What changed after the first move? Notice the outcome of the action. Possible results: progress, a new obstacle, a clue, a cost, a new choice, mission completion.

The result does not have to be dramatic. A small change is enough. Observation prevents the game from becoming a continuous stream of narration without meaningful consequences.

C

Claim the Consequence

Give the result a place in the story. The consequence can be beneficial, neutral, or difficult.

  • The character reaches a safer location.
  • A new route becomes available.
  • The voice reveals a contradiction.
  • The task becomes smaller.
  • The character loses time.
  • A useful fact is discovered.

A consequence should follow the action and shape what happens next. In a real-life task, the consequence can be the actual result of your work.

K

Keep Going or Close

Decide whether the mission continues or ends.

Continue when: the target has not been reached; a new choice has emerged; the character has encountered a new obstacle; you want to explore the next scene.

Close when: the checkable end state has been achieved; the scene has reached a satisfying stopping point; you need to return attention to the real environment; you choose to stop playing.

A mission does not need to continue indefinitely. Completion is a valid outcome.

SECTION 3

The City Is the Real Environment

The city is generated from the surroundings you encounter. You do not need a prepared map. A real object, sound, surface, or landmark can receive fictional meaning.

  • A bus becomes a ferry.
  • A laundromat becomes an alchemy lab.
  • A puddle becomes a moat.
  • A traffic light becomes an omen.
  • A familiar building becomes a safehouse.
  • A keyboard becomes a terminal interface.
  • A compiler error becomes a security alarm.

The sensory detail remains real. The fictional interpretation adds narrative meaning. You can play with what you notice without needing to search for additional scenery.

Sensory Anchoring

Begin a scene by noticing one real sensory detail.

  • Rain against a window.
  • The vibration of a bus.
  • The texture of a countertop.
  • The sound of footsteps.
  • The pressure of your feet against the ground.
  • The sound of a keyboard.
  • The rhythm of your own breathing.

Use a detail that is accessible and comfortable in your current environment. The anchor does not need to be visual. Sound, touch, movement, temperature, and bodily sensation can all establish a scene.

A NOTE FROM THE CORE RULEBOOK This is the same instinct behind the core game's Rule of Threes — one light, one sound, one smell per location. The Portable Protocol just runs it on a real street instead of an imagined one. The city was never optional scenery in CAPSLOCK. It was always the thing actually in front of you.
SECTION 4

The Three-Beat Scene

For a quick scene, use three beats.

Beat 1: Anchor

Notice one real sensory detail.

Rain strikes the windshield.

Beat 2: Argue

Let the voice challenge the character's want. The voice introduces doubt, temptation, fear, memory, or contradiction.

Voice: "You should have stayed home."

Beat 3: Move

The character takes a first move. End with a question, a consequence, or a new detail.

Mara turns onto the side street.
The side street is flooded.

The scene can end there or continue with a new choice. The three beats are a compact storytelling loop. You can repeat them as long as the activity and your attention allow.

SECTION 5

The Voice

The voice is the primary NPC. You do not need multiple characters to create dialogue or conflict.

The voice can represent: the Critic, the Tempter, the Memory, the Doubt, the Witness, the Future, the Unknown.

The voice can speak in second person.

"You know this will fail."

"You have been here before."

"Keep walking. The city is listening."

"You already know the answer."

The voice does not have to be correct. It can contradict the character, reveal useful information, distort memory, or create a question. The player decides how the character responds. The voice creates tension. The character's actions determine what happens next.

FIELD NOTE Runners who've read both documents tend to notice the same thing: the Voice's roster — Critic, Tempter, Memory, Doubt, Witness, Future, Unknown — sounds a great deal like the six Inner Deceivers from a different part of the CAPSLOCK canon, just without their formal names attached. Whether that's the same thing wearing a lighter coat, or two people independently noticing the same handful of voices everyone actually has, is not a question this document tries to settle.
SECTION 6

The Consequence Loop

A playable scene needs more than a first move. It needs a result. After an action, ask: What changed? Then choose a response.

  • Progress — the character moves closer to the target. "The next intersection is clear."
  • New Obstacle — the first move reveals another problem. "The side street is flooded."
  • Cost — the character loses something meaningful. "The Inspector is now closer."
  • Clue — the character discovers information. "A symbol appears on the wall."
  • New Choice — the character must decide between different approaches. "Cross the flooded street or search for another route."
  • Mission Complete — the target has been reached. "The character reaches the river."

The result becomes the starting point for the next moment. This creates a repeating cycle:

Want. Obstacle. Choice. Action. Consequence. Next choice.
SECTION 7

Modes of Play

CAPSLOCK adapts to the activity you are already doing.

COMMUTE

Linear, time-limited

Each stop, segment, or transition becomes a scene boundary. Use Watcher or Enforcer when appropriate. The real journey remains the priority. Example: "The character must reach the next stop before the Inspector notices the signal." A safe physical journey is sufficient — fictional progress does not require additional movement or risk.

CHORE

Repetitive actions as beats

The complete chore is the main mission; individual actions can provide optional scene moments. Example: Target — clear the kitchen counter. First move — pick up one dish. Consequence — a hidden message is discovered beneath it. Reward — the counter becomes clearer. Allow the work to remain ordinary.

NIGHT WALK

Atmosphere and discovery

Each block or transition can introduce a new detail. Hunter obstacles can be approached through workarounds. Real-world navigation and hazard awareness always take priority over the fiction. The story never requires an unsafe detour, crossing, investigation, or physical action.

BEFORE SLEEP

One quiet scene, minimal stakes

The voice can introduce a question or unresolved image. Example: "The door is opening. Who is on the other side?" The scene can end without resolving the question — the purpose is a gentle stopping point, not an unfinished obligation.

PROGRAMMING

The codebase as the city

A function becomes a location. A dependency becomes an organization. A failing test becomes an alarm. Keep the technical goal concrete. Example: Target — understand why process_request calls validate_input. Obstacle — the call path is unclear. Adversary — Hunter. First move — search for references. Consequence — a caller in another module is discovered. Reward — the call path is clearer. The fictional interpretation should support investigation, not replace technical verification.

SECTION 8

Continuity Without Paper

CAPSLOCK is designed to work without written records. Use three optional continuity anchors:

  • One character want.
  • One unresolved question.
  • One recurring voice or phrase.

A landmark can also help you resume a scene. Example:

Mara wants to reach the river. The flooded side street remains unresolved. The voice keeps saying, "You are already too late."

When you return, resume from the last remembered point. If you forget details, you can: reconstruct the scene, simplify the save-file, treat the missing memory as a story gap, or start a new scene with the same character.

Forgetting does not invalidate the game. Continuity supports play. It does not control access to play.

SECTION 9

Immediate Rewards

Close a completed beat with a small, immediate reward.

In fiction: a clue, a breath, a name, a safe location, a moment of clarity.

In real life: a sip of water, a stretch, a moment of rest, a physical acknowledgment of completion, a brief pause before the next activity.

The reward should match the scale of the accomplishment. A reward is an optional closure signal, not a requirement to force productivity.

SECTION 10

Complete Portable Example

Mission Save-File:

Mara wants to reach the river before the Inspector in the flooded city, while the voice says she is already too late.
Anchor
Rain strikes the windshield.
Check
Mara must get through the next intersection unseen.
Acknowledge
The Inspector's car is behind her.
Pick
Watcher. The obstacle is surveillance and avoidance.
Set Stakes
If she is seen, the package may open.
Launch
Mara turns onto the side street.
Observe
The street is flooded.
Claim
The route is blocked. She must choose another approach.
Keep / Close
The mission continues.
Voice
"You should have stayed home."
Next question
Is there another way through?

The scene can resume from the flooded street. The player does not need to plan the entire story in advance.

SECTION 11

The Final Rule

Do not sit down to play. Start with the next first move.

Notice one sensory detail. Let the voice argue. Take an action. Observe what changes. Continue or close.

The city will fill in as you move through it.

CAPSLOCK is a portable mission protocol for turning ordinary moments into playable narrative.
