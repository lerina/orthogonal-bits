SECTION 1

Core Philosophy

CAPSLOCK runs on four principles:

  • Everything fits in your head. No stat blocks, no tracking sheets. If it can't be remembered in a sentence, it's too complicated.
  • Consequences over numbers. Nothing has hit points. Things are either fine, Glitched, or gone — and failure always pushes the story forward instead of stopping it.
  • The world only exists where you're looking. Nothing is planned in advance. You invent the next room the moment you turn the corner.
  • Solo by default. One person can run the whole game — protagonist, world, and the uncertainty that makes it a game and not just daydreaming. A second player is a welcome option, never a requirement.
SECTION 2

Core Resolution System

CAPSLOCK runs on a single randomizer: the Digit-Sum Draw. It replaces dice entirely, needs nothing but a clock (or your own pulse), and reads out a six-shade result — closer to "clean win / win with a cost / clean loss / loss that escalates," with two shades in between, than a flat coin flip.

The Digit-Sum Draw

Step 1 — State the action and set a Difficulty: Easy / Medium / Hard, based on how the fiction is stacked. This is a narrative lever, not a probability you calculate — see the shift rule below.

Step 2 — Get a digit. Check a clock. Take the current second (0–59) and add its two digits together. Keep summing until one digit remains (0–9). :47 → 4+7=11 → 1+1=2.

No clock on hand? The Pulse Draw: count heartbeats from the moment you state the action until you silently think "now." Add the digits of that count together until one digit remains. Same pipeline, no clock required.

Step 3 — convert the digit to a D6
DigitD6

Step 4 — read the D6 as an outcome
D6Outcome
1Yes, and — success, plus something extra.
2Yes — clean success.
3Yes, but — success, at a cost.
4No, but — failure, not a total loss.
5No — failure.
6No, and — failure that escalates.

Step 5 — apply the Difficulty shift: if you called it Easy, shift the outcome one step toward "Yes, and." If Hard, shift one step toward "No, and." Medium — no shift, read the table as-is.

Style's once-per-session upgrade (Section 3) stacks as an extra Easy-tier shift on top of whatever Difficulty you called — a good scene can earn both.

Duet Mode (optional, 2 players)

Add a second person any time you want company. Instead of a clock, the second person silently picks a digit 0–9 and reveals it the moment the player finishes stating the action — their digit runs through the exact same D6 table and outcome table above. Nothing else about CAPSLOCK changes; the second person is just another way to generate the digit, and can default to narrating Enforcers and Hunters if they'd rather be a co-GM than a fellow player.

SECTION 3

Character Creation

Characters are built from three portable pieces: Archetype, Gear, and Cyberware — plus a fourth piece, Style, which gives every character a distinct voice without adding anything to track.

The Rule of Three

Every character carries: 3 Gear Slots, 2 Cyberware Tags, 1 Style. That's it. If you can't remember your whole character in one breath, trim it down.

Style — Give Your Character a Voice

Style is one phrase describing how your character does things, not what they do. Pick one, or invent your own: Reckless, Cold & Calculated, Charismatic Wreck, Old-School, Corpo Defector.

Mechanically: once per session, if your narration truly embodies your Style, you get the Easy-tier shift described in Section 2 — free, on top of whatever Difficulty you called. (In Duet Mode, the second person makes the call instead.)

The "Always On" Rule

Cyberware never burns resources. It's a permanent narrative advantage — always active, no charge to track.

Burn-Out Slots

Gear and cyberware have one state each: Functional or Glitched. A bad hit or a pushed-too-hard use flips it to Glitched. It stays useless until a narrative fix — a back-alley ripperdoc, a scavenged part, a quiet hour of repair.

SECTION 4

The Netrunning Rig

Hacking gets its own lightweight mini-system so digital infiltration feels distinct from physical action, without adding tracking. Your Rig is one sentence, chosen at character creation (Infiltrators start with one free; anyone can jack a rig from a corpse or black market for a Gear slot).

LOUD

"Scavenged military deck — brutal and fast, but loud."

Complications draw attention — a nearby Watcher notices.

UNDERPOWERED

"Boutique stealth deck — quiet, but weak against military ICE."

Complications take time — the guard patrol gets closer.

UNTRACEABLE

"Jury-rigged street deck — cheap, glitchy, impossible to trace."

Complications hit you physically — feedback burns your neural port.

Diving In: entering a system is a single Digit-Sum Draw (or Duet digit, in Duet Mode). Success gets you in. A "No, and" means the ICE spots you immediately — treat it as waking a Hunter.

Inside the Net: don't map the system. Describe only the one node you're touching right now — Fog of War applies to cyberspace exactly like the street.

Burning Out: your Rig can Glitch like any other gear. A Glitched Rig locks you out of your own head for a beat — describe a jarring sensory blackout before you can act again.

SECTION 5

World & Lore Rules

You don't need a setting bible. Build the world exactly as far as your character can currently see.

Rule 1 — One Corp, One Gang, One Bar

Your entire setting only needs three standing entities: The Megacorp (faceless, city-spanning), The Street Gang (immediate, local), The Safe Zone (the one place you can breathe).

Rule 2 — The Rule of Threes (Sensory Anchors)

Any new location gets exactly three sensory details: one light, one sound, one smell. Your brain fills in the rest automatically.

Rule 3 — Progressive Generation (Fog of War)

Nothing exists until it's looked at. When your character turns a corner, decide then what's there.

Rule 4 — "Who Profits?" (Instant Lore)

Any lore question gets answered by asking which Megacorp profits from the current state of things.

SECTION 6

Gear — Expanded Ideas

Gear should be evocative, not itemized. Keep the total at three — swapping mid-session means narrating how you lost or traded the old piece.

  • Weapon Slot: railgun pistol · monofilament whip · EMP grenade (shuts down one piece of tech, no roll needed) · improvised molotov
  • Utility Slot: grapple-hook launcher · portable signal jammer · black-market med-stim (clears one Glitch instantly, once) · lockpick multitool
  • Disguise/Social Slot: stolen corp uniform · forged biometric patch · a favor owed by a fixer · a convincing sob story

SECTION 9

Story Hooks

Pick one, or draw from all three across a session.

1. THE BROKEN UPLINK

Trapped in a Megacorp server room's maintenance shaft with a data drive overheating your neural port on a mental timer, you must bluff past a patrol drone with a stolen uniform and a glitching voice modulator.

2. THE BLACK MARKET DROP

Mid-deal for a prototype cyber-eye in a crowded noodle bar, a warning flashes across your vision: armed corporate agents just walked in the front door. Escape without drawing attention.

3. THE NEON CHASE

You've stolen a luxury flying vehicle, but its autopilot is fighting your controls while interceptor drones close in through smog-choked skyscraper gaps.

SECTION 11

Running the Scene — The Storytelling Backbone

This is the mental shape that turns a Story Hook into an actual scene, without any prep. Use it as your own internal skeleton while you narrate — solo, you're running both the protagonist and the world at once, so this structure is what keeps the two from blurring together.

The shape (used at two zoom levels): whole session — Premise → Intrigue → Conflict → Outcome. Any single beat, zoomed in — Attention → Tension → Transformation → Outcome. Same four steps, twice; zoom into any moment that needs more weight.

Five questions for when a scene stalls

  1. What do they stand to lose that isn't their life?
  2. What's their best flaw and their worst virtue?
  3. What can't be solved by the skill they're proudest of?
  4. What truth would end things if said out loud?
  5. What trope are you already running?

How to connect scenes instead of "and then"

  • Cause: "Which meant that…" / "What nobody saw coming was…"
  • Feeling: "Because she couldn't bear to…" / "The thing he'd never told anyone was…"
  • Echo: "Which was exactly like the time…"
  • Irony: "The very thing that saved her then would destroy her now."
  • Pace: "One heartbeat. Two. Then—"
  • The one that does everything at once: "Which is why, later, when…"

One rule for sensory detail: pick exactly one dominant sense per scene, plus at most one small secondary touch — this folds directly into Rule 2 (Section 5).

SECTION 12

Quest Deconstruction — Turning Any Target into a Quest

A six-step way to take any activity or target — an NPC's goal, a session objective, or something in real life — and shape it into something with actual pull. Reuses the Adversary vocabulary from Section 7.

  1. Name the Target. One concrete sentence with a checkable end state.
  2. Find the Obstacle. Why hasn't this already happened?
  3. Set the Stakes. What actually gets worse if this is skipped?
  4. Pick One Adversary Type. Watcher, Enforcer, or Hunter.
  5. Define the First Move. The next physical action, under two minutes.
  6. Attach a Reward. Something immediate, delivered the instant the target is hit.
Real life: "Reply to that overdue email." Target: reply sent, thread closed. Obstacle: it's awkward. Stakes: the longer it sits, the worse it gets. Adversary type: Watcher. First move: open the draft. Reward: archive the thread.
In-fiction: "Get past the rooftop patrol drone." Target: you're on the far ledge, undetected. Obstacle: the drone's scan sweep every 20 seconds. Stakes: getting spotted wakes the building's Hunter. Adversary type: Watcher. First move: time the sweep. Reward: the crossing cuts straight to the next room.
